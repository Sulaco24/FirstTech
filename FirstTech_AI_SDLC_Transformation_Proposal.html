Chapter 03

Estimation Analysis:
Why the Numbers Moved

Estimates were accurate for the information available at commitment. Changes in scope, requirements, and BA input after commitment caused variance — not weak estimation discipline.

Core principle: An estimate is only as reliable as the inputs behind it. When requirements change late, estimates move — by design. Fix the discovery process, and estimation accuracy follows.

Primary Drivers of Estimation Variance

Scope and requirements were modified after sprint planning — introducing new acceptance criteria, design changes, or business rule updates. Each change invalidated part of the original estimate without a formal re-estimation process.

Clarification requests to BA took multiple days or remained unresolved during the sprint. Developers proceeded with assumptions that later required rework. Faster BA response cycles are the structural fix.

Final design details were provided after stories entered development, revealing additional scope not covered in the original estimate. Design readiness must be a hard gate before sprint commitment.

Integration dependencies, API blockers, and third-party library issues were identified during execution rather than planning — adding 3–7 days of unplanned work per occurrence.

Chapter 05

12 Systemic Blockers
Limiting AI Effectiveness

Portfolio-level SDLC maturity gaps — not stream-specific issues. Each one reduces AI ROI directly.

Requirements arrive incomplete. Stories are written reactively. Thin acceptance criteria force engineers to improvise — AI amplifies this gap, not fixes it.

No consistent link: business need → story → design → test. AI context tools cannot reason reliably over fragmented backlogs.

Scope instability invalidates AI-generated tests and code before it is used. Rework cost is high and largely invisible in velocity metrics.

Stories enter sprints without a readiness threshold. Blockers discovered post-commitment. This is the single highest-leverage process gate to implement.

Each release requires disproportionate manual QA effort. AI-assisted QA tools can't reach potential without an automation foundation. Currently building on Tajeeri — target 54%.

Testing starts in final 40% of the sprint on average. Defects surface too late for same-sprint resolution. Shift-left QA reduces late-sprint spillover by an estimated 30–45%.

Technical dependencies — libraries, APIs, external teams — identified during execution, not planning. Each instance adds 3–7 days of unexpected work.

Copilot and Claude active but without shared prompt practices or output governance. Individual gains not compounding. Estimated value capture: 20–30% of potential.

Engineers rebuild project context manually every session. Tajeeri agents solve this — the model needs replication across streams.

Adoption maturity varies significantly by stream. Without a common framework, best practices don't transfer and portfolio-level AI ROI can't be measured.

Without baseline metrics on spillover, velocity, defect rate and rework, it's impossible to prove AI ROI or justify further investment.

BA capability is inconsistent. Where BA is weak, the requirements bottleneck is structural. AI-assisted BA tooling can help, but cannot replace ownership of the input layer.

Bottom line: More AI tools without fixing these blockers produces the same result as Sprint 10→11 — temporary improvement, then regression. Foundation first.
